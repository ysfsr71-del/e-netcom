How to Read Wildfire Smoke and Air Quality Maps

A media literacy guide to AQI, PM2.5, sensor readings and wildfire smoke maps, with practical verification steps.

What does the map measure?

Not every air-quality map measures the same thing. Some display measured fine-particle (PM2.5) concentrations, some show an Air Quality Index (AQI), and others depict smoke detected or estimated from satellite observations. Do not compare these as if they were identical. AQI translates pollutant levels into public-facing categories; it does not measure an individual's personal exposure.

Check location, time and source

Look for the station's distance from your location, the timestamp, whether readings are instantaneous or averaged, and whether the data come from a regulatory monitor or a lower-cost sensor. Lower-cost sensors can offer useful clues, but humidity, calibration and placement can affect results. Always read the legend and the pollutant represented.

Visible smoke is not the whole risk picture

A clear-looking sky does not guarantee clean air, while a dramatic photograph cannot quantify pollution on its own. Wind and atmospheric conditions can change quickly. When sharing a screenshot, include the date, time, area and source link. Do not present an old map as a current warning.

Do not base health decisions on one graphic

Follow current local authority alerts and public-health guidance. Advice may differ for people more sensitive to air pollution. A social post is not a substitute for official advice. Nor can a single smoke map establish the cause of a wildfire or attribute a specific event to climate change.

Classroom activity: three views of one day

Compare an official monitoring map, a satellite smoke visualization and a social media post for the same area and date. Record data type, time, source, geographic resolution, uncertainty and suggested action. Discuss discrepancies rather than hiding them.
