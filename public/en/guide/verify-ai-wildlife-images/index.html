How to verify AI-generated wildlife images

A striking image of an unfamiliar animal can travel across social media long before anyone checks where it came from. It may be an authentic photograph, an illustration, a composite, or an AI-generated scene. Even a genuine image can be paired with a false date, location, or species name. Verification therefore begins with the claim, not with guessing from pixels.

Identify the claim.

Is the post announcing a newly discovered species, a rare sighting in a particular region, or unusual animal behaviour? Keep the image's origin separate from the caption's assertions. A real image does not make every accompanying statement true.

Trace the source.

Look for the earliest available publication, photographer, date, and location. Reverse-image searches may reveal an earlier version or a different context. Try searching distinctive crops when a full-image search fails. A lack of results is not evidence of authenticity.

Treat visual clues as leads, not verdicts.

Repeated fur patterns, inconsistent shadows, and implausible anatomy may warrant closer examination, but compression, editing, and unusual camera angles can mislead. If provenance records such as Content Credentials are available, inspect what they actually attest to. Their absence does not prove that an image was generated; their presence does not independently verify a wildlife sighting.

Check the ecological context.

IUCN Red List assessments and GBIF occurrence records can help investigate a species' known distribution. Neither database is a complete register of every animal ever observed. An absent record is not definitive disproof, and a plausible range is not proof of the specific photograph. Exceptional claims merit confirmation from researchers, peer-reviewed publications, or responsible conservation organisations.

Share responsibly.

Label unresolved material as unverified rather than presenting it as fact. Avoid publishing precise nesting sites or other sensitive wildlife locations that could expose animals to disturbance or exploitation.

Classroom exercise:

Compare an authentic wildlife photograph, a clearly labelled illustration, and a real photograph paired with a misleading caption. Ask learners to record the claim, available evidence, remaining uncertainty, and what source they would check next. The goal is evidence-based reasoning, not a contest to spot AI artefacts.
