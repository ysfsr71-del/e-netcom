School Biodiversity Surveys: A Responsible Citizen Science Guide

A schoolyard can become a place to notice the living world without disturbing it. This guide offers a careful method for recording observations, checking uncertainty and protecting children's privacy.

3 November 2026 · 8–10 min

Why observe biodiversity?

Biodiversity includes more than a count of species. It also concerns living organisms and the relationships that sustain ecosystems. UNESCO highlights biodiversity's importance for human wellbeing and the role of education in changing how people relate to nature. Even a small schoolyard can offer opportunities to notice plants, birds and insects. A short survey is not a complete inventory: it records only what observers detect during a particular period and under particular conditions.

Choose a safe and inclusive route

Teachers and school managers should approve an accessible route before the activity begins. Check traffic, steep edges, standing water, recently treated vegetation, thorns and allergy concerns. Pupils should not handle wildlife, open nests, pick plants or feed animals. Binoculars, a notebook and photographs taken from a distance are usually enough. Provide weather-appropriate clothing and sun protection. Plan alternative observation positions so students with mobility or sensory needs can participate meaningfully.

Make observations comparable

Select three observation stations and spend approximately ten minutes at each. Record date, time, weather, observation duration and group size. Repeating the same route at similar times makes comparisons more useful. Describe each organism only as precisely as the evidence allows. If the species is uncertain, a description such as 'small brown bird' is more accurate than an unsupported species name. A simple shared form helps prevent missing details and inconsistent counting.

Protect photographs and locations

Images should focus on wildlife rather than identifiable children. Do not publish pupils' names, faces, school security details or precise private addresses. Publicly sharing the exact location of rare or vulnerable organisms may expose them to disturbance or collection. Check platform age requirements, school policies and parental permissions before creating accounts or uploading observations. An offline class spreadsheet is a valid alternative; citizen science does not require publishing every record online.

Treat identification as a hypothesis

Automated image suggestions are not definitive identifications. iNaturalist provides educational guidance on observations and community identification, but similar-looking organisms, season and image quality can all affect results. Compare uncertain records with trustworthy local field guides or a qualified specialist. Keep an 'unidentified' category instead of forcing a species name. Never decide whether a mushroom or plant is safe to touch or eat based on an app's suggestion.

Interpret the limits of the data

Seeing more organisms at one station does not prove that it has a healthier ecosystem. Temperature, rainfall, flowering time, observer experience and the number of visits all influence detection. Keep observation effort separate from the number of organism groups recorded. Not seeing a species does not prove it is absent. Present findings as patterns worth exploring, not as population estimates or definitive evidence of environmental improvement.

A seven-day classroom plan

Day one: agree on safety and privacy rules. Day two: choose stations and prepare the recording form. Days three and four: repeat the observations at the same stations. Day five: compare photographs and notes with a teacher. Day six: mark uncertain identifications and discuss sources of bias. Day seven: prepare a classroom display without personal data or sensitive locations. Repeating the activity in another season can support a more meaningful discussion of change.

Turn attention into careful action

Share results with school staff and consider low-risk improvements: retaining existing vegetation, reviewing unnecessary mowing, removing litter and seeking local ecological advice before planting native species. Introducing unfamiliar plants, relocating nests or applying pesticides is not a pupil activity. Evaluate success through safe participation, consistent records, honest treatment of uncertainty and respect for living things—not simply the number of photographs uploaded.

Reusable classroom observation record

Use the same fields at each observation point: date and time; general site code (not precise coordinates); weather; observation duration in minutes; number of observers; organism group; verified species name if available; number of sightings; identification confidence; teacher review. Do not record pupils' names, identifiable faces or precise locations of sensitive species. Note that the same individual may be counted twice; do not compare raw counts from days with unequal observation effort as though they were equivalent.
