Saving Water at School: Leak Audits and a Measurable Action Plan

Water efficiency should never mean restricting drinking water or handwashing. This guide helps school leaders, facilities staff and educators find avoidable losses through measurement, maintenance and responsible participation.

3 November 2026 · 8–10 min

Water efficiency must protect health

A school's water-efficiency programme must safeguard safe drinking water, handwashing with soap, clean toilets and accessible facilities. WHO's approach to water, sanitation and hygiene in schools connects these services to children's health, dignity and equal participation. Closing drinking-water stations, pressuring pupils to shorten necessary handwashing or cutting toilet cleaning are not legitimate savings. The goal is to remove avoidable waste while preserving dependable services. Before buying equipment or launching a campaign, identify where water is actually being lost.

Build a credible baseline

With school management's approval, collect three to six months of water bills and meter records. Record factors that influence demand: pupil attendance, holidays, kitchen operations, weather and landscape irrigation. Compare daily cubic metres with similar operating days rather than treating summer and winter as interchangeable. EPA WaterSense recommends metering, monitoring and benchmarking for institutional facilities. An audit needs building-level information, not individual children's data. Keep the calculation method and any gaps in the records visible so the results can be checked later.

Map leaks without exposing pupils to hazards

Pupils can tell a teacher about a dripping tap, continuously running toilet, wet floor or leaking garden hose. They should not open service panels, operate valves, remove meters or enter wet areas near electrical equipment. Trained facilities staff can inspect taps, toilet cisterns, connections, tanks and irrigation lines against a documented checklist. Each observation should record location, date, symptom, responsible team and repair status. Avoid publishing photographs that identify children or reveal restricted parts of the building. Reporting is a learning activity; repairs are professional work.

Use meter readings to investigate suspected leaks

At a time when the building is unoccupied and all planned consumption has stopped, authorised staff can record the meter reading and compare it with a later reading. Movement may indicate a leak, automatic tank refill, irrigation or another legitimate demand; one reading alone does not prove a fault. This reflects the leak-checking principle described by EPA WaterSense. Never disable fire protection, healthcare, safety or essential building systems to perform the test. Unexpected results should trigger a documented maintenance investigation, not an improvised repair by students.

Prioritise fixtures and kitchen operations

Fix worn seals, running cisterns and uncontrolled hoses before replacing working equipment. When considering new taps or toilets, assess flow rate, durability, hygiene needs, accessibility, local standards and maintenance capacity together. A certification used in one country does not automatically establish compliance elsewhere. School kitchens must continue all washing and sanitising steps required for food safety. If a dishwasher is available, review loading practices and the manufacturer's instructions. Making sinks difficult to access is not a meaningful conservation outcome.

Review irrigation and cleaning practices

Consider rainfall, plant needs, soil moisture and irrigation-line condition before watering school grounds. If sprinklers run immediately after rain, the authorised team can review their settings. Suitable mulch and locally appropriate planting may reduce irrigation demand, but ecological needs and local restrictions still matter. Where hygiene requirements allow, sweeping a paved area may be preferable to washing it continuously with a hose. Never treat harvested rainwater as drinking water without a properly assessed and approved treatment system. Alternative water supplies require specialist safety review.

Report results without inflated claims

Compare before-and-after consumption under similar conditions. For example, 12 cubic metres in one week versus 10 in another is an illustration, not a measured e-NetCoM result. Without attendance, weather and operational context, the difference cannot establish a reliable savings percentage. Track reported leaks, completed repairs, repair times, and whether drinking water and soap remain available. A lower bill alone is not success if the school has reduced essential services. Keep baseline data and the follow-up date so findings can be reproduced.

A seven-day school water audit

Day one: obtain permission and assign adult responsibilities. Day two: review past meter and billing records. Day three: let pupils flag visible leaks under teacher supervision, without touching plumbing. Day four: have trained staff inspect meters and facilities. Day five: prioritise repairs and efficient fittings against cost, hygiene and accessibility. Day six: explain safe water habits and the leak-reporting route to pupils. Day seven: record findings, owners and a follow-up measurement in one month. This checklist is educational; it is not an instruction to perform plumbing work.

Sources and scope

This educational guide does not replace qualified plumbing, drinking-water safety or public-health advice. Local rules, the school's hygiene plan and authorised facilities personnel take precedence.
