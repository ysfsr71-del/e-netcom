Rain Gardens and Permeable Cities: Where Does Stormwater Go?

Understand rain gardens, urban runoff and permeable paving through sourced explanations, safety guidance and a classroom media-literacy activity.

Where does urban rainwater go?

Roofs, asphalt and concrete limit how much rainfall can soak into the soil. Water may flow into street drains and waterways, carrying litter, sediment, oil and other pollutants. Runoff depends not only on paving but also on storm intensity, slope, soil, drainage capacity and maintenance. A flooded street does not prove that one particular surface caused the flood.

What makes a rain garden different?

A rain garden is a shallow, planted depression designed to receive runoff temporarily from a nearby roof or paved area. With suitable soils, some water can infiltrate; engineered bioretention systems may include an underdrain and a safe overflow. This is a water-management feature, not simply a decorative flowerbed. It cannot guarantee protection from extreme storms.

Green infrastructure works as a system

Permeable paving can let water enter engineered layers below the surface, but its performance depends on suitable design and preventing clogging. Vegetated swales can slow runoff; green roofs can retain some rainfall if structural and waterproofing requirements are met. Rainwater tanks can store collected roof water for appropriate non-drinking uses under local rules. None is a substitute for catchment planning or properly maintained conventional drainage.

A simple rainfall calculation

One millimetre of rainfall across one square metre represents one litre of water. Ten millimetres over a roof with a 100-square-metre horizontal footprint therefore corresponds to a theoretical 1,000 litres of rainfall. This is not the amount that will necessarily reach a tank or drain: losses, storage, duration and the roof system matter. It is a learning example, not an engineering sizing method.

Safety, location and maintenance

Do not excavate near foundations, underground utilities, steep slopes or potentially contaminated ground without professional assessment and permissions. Designers must consider infiltration, safe overflow routes, access, plants and long-term upkeep. Debris or sediment can block inlets and permeable paving. During heavy rain, never enter pooled water or open storm drains; report concerns to responsible adults or authorities.

How to verify impressive claims

A photograph showing a dry road after a storm is not evidence that a rain garden prevented flooding. Ask when and where the image was taken, how much rain fell, which surfaces drained into the feature, where overflow went and whether comparable measurements exist. Claims such as “100% absorption” or “no more flooding” require specific test conditions and cannot be generalized.

Five-day classroom activity

Day 1: map roofs, paved areas and planted ground from a safe location. Day 2: observe runoff from indoors during ordinary rainfall. Day 3: draw possible pathways, marking observation separately from inference. Day 4: calculate the theoretical rainfall volume for a 20-square-metre area receiving 5 mm of rain: 100 litres. Day 5: prepare a proposal for professional review, without digging, redirecting water or testing during storms.
