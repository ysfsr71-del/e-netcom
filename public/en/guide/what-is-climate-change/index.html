What Is Climate Change? Causes, Impacts and Responses

Climate change is more than a series of hot days. This guide explains long-term trends, human influence and practical responses without confusing weather with climate.

Weather versus climate

Weather describes conditions such as temperature, rain and wind at a particular place and time. Climate describes the distribution, averages and extremes of these conditions over many years. One cold day does not overturn a long-term global warming trend, just as one hot afternoon does not establish climate change on its own.

Climate has changed through natural processes in the past, but human greenhouse gas emissions are the principal cause of current rapid warming. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Synthesis Report states that average global surface temperature in 2011–2020 was about 1.1°C above the 1850–1900 reference period. This is a decade-long average, not the temperature anomaly for every individual year.

Why human activities are warming the planet

Carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide contribute to the greenhouse effect by reducing the rate at which heat escapes to space. The natural greenhouse effect makes Earth habitable; the problem is the additional warming caused by increasing concentrations of these gases.

Burning coal, oil and gas is a major source of human carbon dioxide emissions. Land-use change and activities in agriculture, energy and waste systems also contribute. Effective solutions therefore involve electricity generation, buildings, transport and production systems as well as household choices. A single consumer purchase cannot substitute for wider structural changes.

How scientists establish the evidence

Researchers compare several independent lines of evidence: land and ocean temperature records, satellites, ocean heat content, shrinking glaciers and rising sea level. NASA documents how these indicators reinforce one another. A credible explanation should distinguish observations from projections and clearly identify the time period under discussion.

Annual temperature rankings can vary with natural influences such as El Niño and La Niña. Copernicus publishes datasets and long-term climate indicators that help readers examine trends rather than isolated years. When sharing a chart, check its axes, baseline, uncertainty and data source. A cropped graph without context can be misleading even if its underlying measurements are real.

Impacts, exposure and unequal risks

Climate change can influence heat extremes, heavy rainfall, drought and sea-level rise, but impacts differ across locations. It is not scientifically sound to claim that every flood, wildfire or storm was caused solely by climate change. Attribution studies assess how a changing climate affects the likelihood or intensity of specific events.

Hazard, exposure and vulnerability jointly shape risk. Housing quality, access to shade, income, age and health services matter. The World Health Organization identifies extreme heat as a serious and growing health threat. Following official local warnings and checking on vulnerable people are important forms of preparedness, especially during prolonged hot periods.

Mitigation and adaptation: two necessary responses

Mitigation means reducing greenhouse gas emissions or strengthening removals. Examples include energy efficiency, cleaner power, accessible public transport, reducing food waste and extending product life. The size of any benefit depends on local energy sources, infrastructure and life-cycle conditions, so avoid unsupported claims about exact savings.

Adaptation means preparing for changes already occurring or expected: urban shade, water management, flood planning, heat-health action plans and early-warning systems. Neither approach replaces the other. Schools and workplaces can define a baseline, assign responsibility and review progress. An action plan should consider affordability and who may otherwise be left unprotected.

A seven-day climate literacy plan

Day one: explain the difference between weather and climate in your own words. Day two: read the IPCC headline findings. Day three: compare two NASA indicators. Day four: check the baseline and date range of a temperature chart.

Day five: identify one avoidable energy use at home or school. Day six: find your local official heat or flood alert channel. Day seven: choose one mitigation action and one adaptation action, and decide how to track them. Do not invent precise emissions savings where no reliable calculation is available.
