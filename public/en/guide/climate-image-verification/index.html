Verifying Photos and Videos in Climate News: Spotting False Context

1. Start with the claim

Flood, wildfire and storm images spread quickly. A genuine photograph can still mislead when paired with the wrong date or location. Separate what the image depicts from what the caption claims. A single weather event cannot by itself prove or disprove a long-term climate trend.

2. Trace the earliest appearance

Select a distinctive frame and run a reverse-image search. Compare older publications, local news archives and original agency records. The first search result is not necessarily the original source.

3. Check place and time

Signs, landmarks, terrain and weather can provide clues but rarely prove location alone. Upload dates are not capture dates. Social platforms often strip EXIF metadata, so missing metadata is not proof of manipulation.

4. Find independent context

Compare timestamped information from meteorological agencies, emergency services and local authorities. Even when footage belongs to a real disaster, claims about climate change require separate scientific attribution research.

5. Assess AI claims carefully

Visual oddities and AI detectors are fallible. Neither a detector score nor the absence of obvious artifacts settles authenticity. Prioritize provenance, corroboration and transparent uncertainty.

6. Five questions before sharing

Who published it? When did it first appear? Where was it captured? What independent evidence supports it? Does the caption claim more than the image shows?

7. A 20-minute classroom activity

Use two openly licensed, non-graphic images with accurate and misleading captions. In pairs, students trace the sources and write a short verification note distinguishing evidence from uncertainty. Avoid traumatic imagery and personal data.
