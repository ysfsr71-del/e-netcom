A School Library of Things: Sharing Instead of Buying

A practical guide to setting up a safe, inclusive school library of things, with inventory, lending rules and meaningful impact measures.

What is a library of things?

A library of things lets students and staff borrow durable items they need only occasionally. It extends the familiar library model beyond books to magnifying glasses, measuring cups, binoculars and suitable classroom project materials. Its purpose is to make shared access easier than unnecessary individual purchases.

When does sharing help the environment?

Products use resources long before disposal: extraction, manufacturing, packaging and transport all matter. Lending can reduce demand for new products when a loan genuinely replaces a purchase. It would be misleading to claim a fixed carbon saving for every loan; maintenance, travel and product lifetime affect the outcome.

Choose items based on real needs

Ask learners which robust, portable and easy-to-clean items they rarely use. A pilot collection of 10–15 suitable objects can be enough. Magnifiers, rulers and simple garden observation tools may work well. Exclude hazardous chemicals, powered cutting equipment, personal protective equipment and hygiene-sensitive items.

Keep an inventory and clear lending rules

Record each item's ID, condition, age suitability and responsible supervisor. Log the loan date, return deadline and condition at handover. Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information about children. Clean and inspect items after return; favour repair and learning over punitive responses to accidental damage.

Make safety and access non-negotiable

Secure shelves, store heavy objects low and remove damaged equipment. Use legible, high-contrast labels and simple instructions. Ensure the lending point is accessible to wheelchair users and supervise unfamiliar equipment. Review the school's own safeguarding and equipment policies before opening.

Run a four-week pilot

Week one: survey needs and appoint a team. Week two: label items, prepare the inventory and agree on cleaning. Week three: begin supervised loans. Week four: collect feedback and revise the rules. Measure the usefulness of the service, not just the number of objects on the shelf.

Measure outcomes without exaggeration

Track loans, distinct items used, on-time returns, repairs and user satisfaction. An optional anonymous question—'Would you otherwise have bought this item?'—can indicate potential avoided purchases. These answers are estimates, not verified greenhouse-gas savings.

Build a lasting sharing culture

School librarians, teachers, students and families can share responsibilities. Inspect donations as carefully as purchased items and review little-used equipment each month. Explore the Green Guide and e-NetCoM project for related sustainability ideas.
