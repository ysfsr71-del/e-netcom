What Is Environmental Communication?

Turn reliable climate and sustainability evidence into messages that people can understand, question and act on.

Why environmental communication matters

Environmental communication connects evidence about climate change, waste, water, energy and biodiversity with decisions made by communities. Its purpose is not simply to attract attention. People should be able to understand what a claim means, where the evidence comes from and which actions are feasible.

Effective communication is a conversation rather than a stream of announcements. Students, educators and residents need opportunities to ask questions and challenge assumptions. The United Nations recommends grounding climate messages in authoritative science, explaining solutions and relating them to local experiences.

Know your audience and context

Before designing a campaign, identify who needs the information. A school club, a municipal team and a household may face different constraints. Ask what the audience already knows, which languages and formats are accessible, and what resources people can actually use.

A reusable-bottle campaign illustrates the point. Explaining plastic waste is useful, but access to safe drinking-water refill points also matters. Avoid blaming individuals for infrastructure they cannot control. Invite the intended audience to help define both the problem and the possible response.

Evidence, uncertainty and verification

For every numerical claim, record the source, date, unit, location and measurement method. Check whether a chart has a truncated axis or a selectively chosen period. A study from one location cannot automatically describe every community. Acknowledge uncertainty without suggesting that uncertainty makes all evidence worthless.

UNESCO emphasizes access to accurate environmental information as a public good. Verify the original context of photographs, quotations and videos before publishing them. When the evidence is insufficient, say so instead of claiming certainty. Our climate misinformation guide offers a practical verification checklist.

Accessible and responsible storytelling

A poster should have a clear main message, a readable heading and a source that supports the visual. Charts need units and explanatory captions. Add meaningful alternative text to informative images and captions to videos; do not rely on colour alone to convey important distinctions. Avoid unnecessary jargon.

A constant stream of disaster imagery can leave audiences feeling powerless. Present risks accurately while also showing credible solutions and local participation. Do not portray communities only as victims. Invite them to explain their own knowledge and priorities. A simple verified chart is often more useful than an impressive but untraceable photograph.

Plan a campaign and measure results

Consider a school food-waste project. First measure a baseline, such as the mass of cafeteria waste over one week, while documenting how measurements were made. Agree on an achievable goal and provide a way for students to respond. Measure again with the same method, noting changes in attendance or circumstances.

Reach, likes and views are communication metrics, not proof of environmental benefit. Knowledge, participation, behaviour and waste reduction require different measures. UNEP's guidance on sustainability campaigns and its communication playbook help teams avoid misleading claims and messages that encourage unnecessary consumption.

A seven-day practice plan

Day 1: choose one environmental issue and a clearly defined audience. Day 2: gather information from at least two credible sources. Day 3: ask people about practical barriers. Day 4: draft one clear message and an accessible visual.

Day 5: test the draft with a small group and correct misunderstandings. Day 6: publish through an appropriate channel and invite questions. Day 7: assess feedback and, where possible, a relevant behaviour measure. Report uncertainty and never claim an environmental impact that has not been measured.
