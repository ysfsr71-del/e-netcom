Tire and road wear: Pollution beyond the tailpipe

Learn about non-exhaust traffic particles, rainwater transport and how to verify environmental claims.

Traffic-related particles do not all come from exhaust. Contact between tires and pavement can release material from both surfaces; brake wear and resuspended road dust are other non-exhaust sources. These categories are not interchangeable. Not every particle on a road is tire-derived, and not every airborne particle should be labelled a microplastic.

Where can the particles go?

Some material can become airborne, while other material settles beside roads. Rainwater may move deposited particles into drainage networks and, depending on local infrastructure, receiving waters. Transport depends on particle properties, rainfall and the built environment. A claim that a vehicle produces no particulate pollution simply because it has no tailpipe is misleading: electric vehicles still use tires and brakes. Equally, comparisons between vehicle types require attention to mass, driving patterns and braking systems rather than a single universal number.

Practical responses

Avoiding unnecessary abrupt acceleration and braking and following manufacturer guidance on tire inflation and maintenance are sensible safety and wear-management practices. Safe public transport, walking and cycling options can help reduce vehicle travel overall. Follow local road-safety rules and qualified maintenance advice; this article is not a vehicle-service manual.

Media-literacy exercise

Compare two posts: “All road dust is plastic” and “Zero tailpipe emissions means zero particle emissions.” Ask what material was actually measured, by which method, in which place and year, and with what uncertainty. Discuss why a photograph of roadside dust cannot establish its chemical composition. Do not collect samples in traffic or near active roads.
