How to Read an Environmental Product Declaration (EPD)

Standards update (November 2026):

ISO 14025:2026 is the published second edition; ISO 14025:2006 has been withdrawn. Check the issue date, programme rules and verification information of each individual EPD.

“Eco-friendly” on a package does not identify what was measured. An Environmental Product Declaration (EPD) presents quantified environmental information under defined product category rules and a life-cycle assessment framework. EPDs are common for construction products. They are disclosures, not automatic awards proving that a product is environmentally superior in every context.

Check the declared unit.

Read the product identity, manufacturing context, validity period, programme operator, reference standard and functional or declared unit. One kilogram of material is not automatically comparable with one square metre of a product delivering a specified service life.

Read the system boundary.

A declaration may cover raw-material supply and manufacturing, while use, maintenance and end-of-life stages may be reported separately or excluded. A cradle-to-gate result should not be advertised as a whole-life result.

Look beyond one carbon figure.

Climate indicators matter, but water, resource use and other impact categories may also be relevant. Values depend on assumptions, data quality, energy mix and the version of the calculation method.

Avoid misleading rankings.

Different product category rules, boundaries, functions, lifetimes or methods can make side-by-side comparisons invalid. Check functional equivalence and methodological compatibility before claiming one product is “30% greener.”

Media-literacy exercise.

When an advertisement says “EPD certified, therefore zero emissions,” open the declaration and inspect the unit, impact indicators, boundaries and verification statement. Having an EPD does not mean zero emissions. Students can highlight the included life-cycle stages in two declarations.
