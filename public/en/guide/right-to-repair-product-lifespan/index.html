Right to Repair: Why Extending Product Life Matters

Buying a replacement phone or appliance can feel easier than repairing one. Yet the purchase price tells only part of the story: manufacturing uses materials and energy, while disposal generates waste. Repairability describes whether a product can be restored safely and affordably after a fault. Repair is not always the best option; the goal is to avoid treating replacement as the only possible response.

What makes a product repairable?

Consider access to spare parts and instructions, disassembly, battery replacement, software support and repair costs together. A single score or marketing claim cannot describe the entire useful life of a device.

What is changing in the EU?

Directive (EU) 2024/1799 introduces measures to promote repair for specified goods. Its product scope and national implementation timetable matter. It should not be presented as automatically creating identical consumer rights in every country, including Türkiye.

Five questions before buying:

Are spare parts available? Can authorised or independent repairers service it? Can the battery be replaced? How long will security updates continue? How does the total repair cost compare with replacement?

Media literacy check:

Question absolute claims such as “unrepairable”, “zero waste” or “lasts forever”. Environmental performance depends on use, maintenance, energy demand and end-of-life conditions. A slogan is not a life-cycle assessment.

Action today:

Look up the model number of one device you own and check official repair and software-support information. Do not attempt to repair swollen batteries, damaged mains wiring or high-voltage components yourself.

How to compare repair quotes

Ask repair providers for a written description of the fault, parts to be used, labour costs, estimated turnaround, data-protection measures and post-repair warranty terms. Check that competing quotes cover the same work. If a seller says a product “cannot be repaired”, request the technical reason. Stop using a device if repair may be unsafe and seek qualified advice.

Unlike a general guide to electronic waste, this article focuses on evidence-based consumer repair decisions and scrutiny of repairability claims. The EU Directive's scope and implementation dates must be checked for the relevant product and jurisdiction.
