A School Seed-Sharing Library: A Safe, Practical Guide

What is a school seed library?

A school seed library is a supervised learning collection where pupils identify, store, sow and sometimes share suitable seeds. It is not an invitation to distribute unknown plants. Its value lies in observing plant life cycles, discussing biodiversity and recording evidence. Even a classroom with only a few pots can participate; a large garden is not essential.

1. Approve a safe species list first

A teacher and the school should select clearly identified species appropriate to local growing conditions. Reject unidentified, toxic, potentially invasive or otherwise unsafe seeds. Do not collect from protected areas or private land without permission. Check local seed-sharing rules and any restrictions attached to commercial varieties. When uncertain, consult a qualified local horticultural adviser before accepting a batch.

2. Start with a manageable collection

Choose two or three familiar species for the first term and keep quantities small. Reused clean paper envelopes, a sturdy box, a notebook and a dry storage container are enough. Mark the collection 'seeds for planting — not food'. Adults must supervise handling, particularly with younger children, and pupils should wash their hands after handling plant material.

3. Make every envelope traceable

Record the common and, where possible, scientific name; variety if known; origin; harvest or packing date; approximate quantity; recommended sowing season; and the supervising adult. Never mix batches of uncertain origin. Explain that seeds from hybrid plants may not reproduce the parent plant's traits. Avoid putting children's personal details on publicly visible labels.

4. Harvest and store with care

Collect mature seed from healthy plants in dry weather. Dry only those species that tolerate dry storage; some seeds must be sown promptly. For dry-storable seeds, use labelled paper envelopes inside a cool, dry, dark container. Moisture and warmth can reduce viability and encourage mould. Check packages regularly; isolate any batch showing pests, mould or an unusual smell.

5. Share fairly, not competitively

A simple ledger can track dates, species, envelopes received and issued, planting notes and feedback. Do not require every pupil to return seed: home growing space and resources differ. Arrange supervised collection times and modest per-person quantities. Never turn the activity into unsupervised release of seeds into parks or natural habitats.

6. Test germination and discuss uncertainty

Select a small sample, such as ten seeds from one identified batch, and germinate them under suitable conditions. If eight sprout, the observed germination rate is 80%; it is not a guarantee for every seed in storage. Record temperature, dates and care so classes can compare observations responsibly. A failed test is useful evidence, not a reason to conceal results.

7. A four-week school plan

Week one: choose species and assess safety. Week two: label envelopes and set up the log. Week three: run small germination trials and sow suitable seeds. Week four: review observations, correct errors and assign responsibility for holidays. Keep the scheme small until the school can maintain accurate records.

8. Measure learning, not just distribution

Count verified species, correctly labelled batches, completed germination tests and pupils involved. Compare outcomes with the initial plan rather than celebrating the largest number of envelopes. Plant only in approved pots or school plots; consult local experts before using any seed in wider habitats.

Frequently asked questions

Can any seed be exchanged? No: identity, safety, origin and local rules matter. How long do seeds keep? It varies by species and storage conditions; retest germination. Is a garden required? No: supervised pot growing can work well.
