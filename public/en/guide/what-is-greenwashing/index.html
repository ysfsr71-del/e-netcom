What Is Greenwashing? A Practical Guide to Checking Green Claims

A product described as “eco-friendly” is not automatically proven sustainable. Check what is measured, what is excluded and who verifies the claim.

What greenwashing means

Greenwashing is communication that gives a misleadingly positive impression of a product's or organisation's environmental performance. Even a real improvement can mislead when it is presented as proof that all operations are environmentally sound. The United Nations highlights vague language, overstated minor improvements and the omission of other impacts as common warning signs.

A green colour palette, leaf symbol or nature photograph is not evidence of environmental performance. Ask which product, process, time period and environmental effect the statement covers. Missing evidence does not by itself prove deliberate deception: it is more accurate to say that the claim remains unverified.

Define the scope of a claim

When an advert says “30% fewer emissions”, ask “Compared with what?” Is the baseline last year's model, the company's previous operations or a competitor? Without a clear unit, reference year and scope, even a numerical claim may omit crucial context. An improvement in manufacturing does not automatically eliminate impacts from transport or use.

“Made with recycled content” describes a material attribute, not the full environmental footprint. Packaging, durability, repairability, useful life and end-of-life treatment also matter. The UN recommends examining impacts throughout a product's life cycle rather than treating one positive attribute as a complete verdict.

Labels, evidence and comparisons

Find out who awarded an environmental label, which criteria it assesses and whether the certification is publicly documented. A badge designed by a brand is not equivalent to an independently audited certification. Independent verification is not an absolute guarantee either: read its scope, methodology and expiry date.

The US Federal Trade Commission's Green Guides summary warns that broad, unqualified claims such as “green” or “eco-friendly” are difficult to substantiate. Specific and clearly qualified benefits are more useful. These US guidelines are not automatically binding law outside their jurisdiction; they provide a practical framework for questioning advertising.

Net-zero and future promises

A promise to reach net-zero emissions in 2050 is different from measurable reductions today. Examine interim milestones, the emissions covered, annual progress and independent review. Carbon credits or offsetting should not be treated as an automatic substitute for reducing emissions from the underlying activity.

EU Directive 2024/825 strengthens consumer protection against misleading environmental claims. Its application date in EU member states is 27 September 2026. This is an EU legal framework: do not assume that the same enforcement rules apply directly to advertising in other countries. Local law must be considered separately.

Checking advertising and social posts

Look closely at adverts that pair environmental imagery with qualifications in tiny print. Does “plastic-free” refer to the packaging or the product? What measurable benefit does “natural” actually promise? A percentage with no accessible evidence or methodology is a reason to pause before sharing.

Rather than accusing a company without sufficient evidence, save the full advertisement, its date and any supporting sustainability report. Trace the claim to primary documentation, record the baseline and the limits of the statement, and then compare it with independent evidence. Critical media literacy protects against both misleading green claims and unsupported accusations.

A seven-day verification exercise

Day 1: choose one environmental advert. Day 2: save the exact claim and visual. Day 3: identify the product and environmental impact being described. Day 4: look for the method, baseline year and any certification. Keep the original context rather than relying on a cropped screenshot.

Day 5: compare the statement with an independent source. Day 6: note potential life-cycle impacts omitted from the advert. Day 7: write a short assessment with three headings: “Verified”, “Unclear” and “Evidence still needed”. If the available material cannot support a conclusion, state that uncertainty plainly.
