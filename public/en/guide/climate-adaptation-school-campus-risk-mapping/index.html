Climate Adaptation at School: A Practical Campus Risk-Mapping Guide

Climate adaptation is more than redesigning buildings. Students, educators and school leaders can identify local exposures together and propose safer, accessible improvements without confusing observations with professional safety assessments.

3 November 2026 · about 10 minutes

Adaptation and mitigation have different purposes

Mitigation aims to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. Adaptation aims to limit harm from climate impacts that are already occurring or expected. An energy-efficient school can contribute to mitigation, while shade, suitable ventilation and reliable drinking water can help it cope with hot days. The IPCC explains climate risk through the interaction of hazards, exposure and vulnerability. A regional climate projection is therefore a useful starting point, not a structural safety certificate for a particular school building.

Ask three questions: what, where and for whom?

Begin with a hazard such as prolonged heat, intense rainfall, drought or storms. Next, identify possible exposure: an uncovered waiting area, a flooded entrance or a classroom that becomes too hot. Then consider vulnerability: limited shade, inaccessible routes or difficulty reaching drinking water may increase the consequences. Record the date, source and uncertainty of each observation. Do not label a place 'safe' or 'unsafe' on the basis of a student survey; qualified professionals must assess engineering, electrical and fire-safety questions.

Collect evidence without collecting personal data

An approved campus sketch, public alerts from the national meteorological service, observations of shade and general information provided by maintenance staff may be enough for a first exercise. If you measure something, record the method, time and limitations; never invent temperatures or risk scores. Do not publish children's faces, medical details, timetables or security equipment. Observation should take place only in permitted, accessible areas under adult supervision. Roofs, electrical panels, plant rooms and flooded surfaces are not appropriate learning sites.

Heat: shade, indoor conditions and access

Heat exposure depends on more than outdoor air temperature. Direct sun, building materials, ventilation, time of day and places to rest all matter. A class can map shaded routes, drinking-water points and waiting areas exposed to the sun without entering restricted spaces. School leaders can ask qualified staff to consider suitable shade and maintenance improvements. Students should not modify electrical devices, window safety mechanisms or building elements. During extreme-heat alerts, instructions from public-health and education authorities take precedence over a classroom project.

Consider heavy rain and drought separately

Heavy rain may disrupt access when water collects at entrances, drains cannot cope or paths become slippery. Drought can place different pressures on irrigation, hygiene and water availability. Both hazards may affect the same campus at different times, so a single unexplained 'climate score' hides important differences. Students may observe runoff from a safe distance after rainfall, but drain cleaning, flood protection and rainwater systems are tasks for authorised teams. Harvested rainwater must not be presented as drinking water without legal, technical and hygiene safeguards.

Inclusion and privacy are part of resilience

Young children, people with limited mobility and learners with visual or hearing impairments may face different barriers along the same route. Accessible alternatives, understandable notices and places to rest can improve the experience of the entire school community. Nobody should be required to disclose a disability or medical history for the project. Needs should be addressed through appropriate school channels while protecting privacy. UNESCO's whole-institution approach connects climate learning with facilities, governance and community partnerships rather than treating it as a one-off lesson.

Turn observations into accountable decisions

A simple table can have five fields: observation, affected activity, date and evidence, responsible team, and verification status. 'No shade at the lunchtime queue' is an observation; 'the building is structurally unsafe' is not a conclusion a student team can draw. Even low-risk improvements need approval from responsible staff, while construction requires professional design, budgeting and maintenance decisions. The UNDRR school safety framework connects safe learning facilities, school safety management and risk-reduction education. Keep unresolved questions visible rather than filling gaps with guesses.

A seven-day school adaptation activity

Day 1: agree permissions and boundaries. Day 2: collect public local hazard information. Day 3: observe shade, water access and routes in safe areas. Day 4: add dated findings to an approved map. Day 5: list missing information and items requiring specialist review. Day 6: check accessibility and privacy, then send proposals to responsible staff. Day 7: agree a review date and a modest process indicator, such as the number of waiting areas examined. This exercise does not prove that climate risks have disappeared.

Sources and limitations

This educational planning resource is not an engineering inspection, an official emergency plan, a building-safety judgement or evacuation guidance. Structural defects, electrical hazards and flood safety require assessment by authorised professionals.
