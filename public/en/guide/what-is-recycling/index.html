What Is Recycling? How It Works and How to Sort Correctly

Recycling processes suitable discarded materials into inputs for new products. But not every item is accepted locally, and prevention and reuse should come first.

The three stages of recycling

Recycling involves collecting suitable materials, sorting and processing them, and using recovered materials to make new products. The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) describes three stages: collection and processing, manufacturing, and purchasing products made with recycled content.

Putting a package in a recycling bin does not guarantee that it will become a new product. Material type, contamination, processing capacity and demand for recovered materials all matter. “Recyclable” is not the same as “actually recycled”.

Why prevention and reuse come first

The European Commission's waste hierarchy prioritises prevention, then preparation for reuse, followed by recycling. Avoiding an unnecessary package or keeping a working device in use can prevent waste at its source.

Repairing, sharing or responsibly donating a usable product is often worth considering before disposal. Recycling is important, but it should not be presented as a substitute for reducing consumption.

Paper, glass, metal and plastic differ

Paper and cardboard: often accepted, although heavy food contamination and some coatings may be a problem. Glass: bottles and jars are not always collected with ceramics or mirrors. Metal: suitable packaging can be recovered, but local sorting instructions vary.

Plastics: a number inside a triangle usually identifies the resin type; it does not guarantee local acceptance. Films, foam and compostable plastics may need separate handling. EPA's material-by-material guide recommends checking your local collection programme.

Contamination and battery safety

Incorrect materials and heavy food residues can lower the quality of collected recyclables. Empty containers and follow local instructions on preparation. Avoid wasting large amounts of water on unnecessary washing.

Lithium-ion batteries can cause fires if damaged in ordinary recycling equipment. EPA recommends specialist collection rather than household recycling bins. Electronics, medicines and hazardous chemicals may also require separate routes. Follow current guidance from your local authority or authorised collection service.

Better systems at school and work

Start by identifying common waste streams and checking what is genuinely collected. Label containers with readable words and examples rather than colour alone. Place them where waste is generated, and coordinate with cleaning and collection teams.

During a short pilot, record amounts collected and sorting mistakes. e-NetCoM's environmental education activities and E-Merkez directory can support environmental literacy projects.

A seven-day sorting checklist

  1. Day 1: read your municipality's collection rules.
  2. Day 2: identify your most common packaging materials.
  3. Day 3: avoid one unnecessary packaging purchase.
  4. Day 4: repair or share a usable item.
  5. Day 5: find a safe battery and electronics drop-off point.
  6. Day 6: improve sorting labels at home or school.
  7. Day 7: review mistakes and adjust the system.

Success is not simply more material in a bin: waste prevented, correct sorting and safe collection all matter.
